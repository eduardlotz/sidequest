# Quest Deck Design Document
## Helping Players Start the Right Game

---

# Vision

The goal of this project is **not** to replace achievements or create random challenges.

The goal is to solve one specific problem:

> "I own 300 games and don't know what I want to play."

The deck should reduce decision paralysis by giving players a compelling reason to launch one of the games they already own.

Every card should make the player think:

> "Oh... this game would be perfect for this."

Instead of

> "I guess every game technically fits."

---

# The Core Design Principles

There are four principles every card should follow.

## 1. Inspire a Session

Cards should encourage an enjoyable gaming session, not a tiny task.

Bad:

- Kill 10 enemies
- Jump off a mountain
- Craft an item

Good:

- Discover somewhere you've never been.
- Unlock something permanent.
- Become noticeably stronger.
- Finish something you've been avoiding.

The objective should naturally last **20–60 minutes**.

---

## 2. Be Game-Agnostic

A card should work in many games while still feeling meaningful.

Instead of

> Defeat the first boss.

Use

> Defeat an opponent you've been avoiding.

Examples:

Elden Ring
→ Beat Margit

Monster Hunter
→ Hunt the monster you've skipped

Diablo
→ Clear a difficult dungeon

Hades
→ Beat the boss that usually stops your run

Every player interprets the quest differently.

---

## 3. Evoke a Feeling

Players don't choose genres.

Players choose experiences.

Nobody thinks:

> "I want to play an Action RPG."

They think:

- I want to relax.
- I want exploration.
- I want to feel powerful.
- I want a challenge.
- I want something creative.

The deck should target emotions instead of genres.

---

## 4. Reward Meaningful Progress

Every completed card should leave the save file in a better place.

Good outcomes:

- new weapon
- new ability
- new area
- new story chapter
- new base upgrade
- new achievement
- new companion
- new resource

Avoid objectives that disappear immediately.

---

# Session Philosophy

Every card should satisfy one of five player motivations.

## Discover

See something new.

---

## Improve

Become stronger.

---

## Create

Leave the world different.

---

## Overcome

Beat something difficult.

---

## Experience

Enjoy the atmosphere.

If a card doesn't satisfy one of these motivations, reconsider whether it belongs in the deck.

---

# Avoid Generic Micro-Tasks

Don't create cards like

- Kill an enemy
- Jump
- Loot a chest
- Craft one item
- Walk 1000 meters

They're completed accidentally.

Players don't remember them.

---

Instead create memorable sessions.

Good examples

- Reach a place you've always wanted to visit.
- Finish a quest you've ignored for weeks.
- Unlock a permanent upgrade.
- Replace one piece of equipment with something better.
- Learn a mechanic you've never mastered.
- Beat an encounter that previously defeated you.
- Improve your home, base, or settlement.
- Follow your curiosity without checking guides.
- Collect something you'll still use ten hours from now.
- Change your character in a meaningful way.

# The Golden Rule

Every card should answer one simple question:

> **"Which game in my library would make this objective the most fun right now?"**

If a player immediately thinks of one or two games, the card is successful.

If every game fits equally well—or none stand out—the card is too generic.

The deck should never tell the player exactly what to do. Instead, it should provide just enough direction to remove the friction of choosing a game, while leaving enough freedom for the player to interpret the objective within the unique systems of the game they decide to play.
